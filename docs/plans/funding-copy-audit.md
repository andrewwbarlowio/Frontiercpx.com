# Funding Solutions: editorial audit and rewrite brief

17 November 2026. Scope: current funding-products-restructure branch, including all 21 Funding Solutions destinations (directory, 12 new product/hub pages and eight retained funding pages). Sector pages excluded from this audit. This is a critical assessment, not a claim of completed rewriting. No customer-facing copy changed by this report.

## Overall judgement

The navigation now exposes the product range, but the copy is not yet strong enough for a release-quality funding proposition. It gives broadly sensible introductory mechanics while leaving too much of the actual customer decision unexplained. It repeats the language of assessing and organising a requirement, rather than demonstrating why an owner or finance director would choose a product and why Frontier helps.

The problem is substantive density, not a universal word shortage. New product pages spread short explanations across seven large sections. Older funding pages are longer, but repeat readiness, hypothetical briefs, general process and cautionary wording. Filling the same template is not the same as answering the customer's questions.

Repeated headings include What could this support?, From the requirement to the right structure., Understand the whole agreement., and Bring the details that shape the decision. These are not individually wrong; repeated across most new pages, they create an interchangeable reading experience. The same production-floor image appears on ten of the twelve new pages. That reinforces the sense of a template and is particularly weak for vehicle, trade and invoice finance.

The copy is educational but commercially hesitant. Phrases about provider conversations, assessment and defining a requirement recur without explaining the practical value of Frontier's introduction. Disclosures about fees and sharing information appear in multiple places. Necessary accuracy should remain, but uncertainty should be attached to specific eligibility or terms, rather than weakening the basic explanation everywhere.

## What the Lombard comparison actually establishes

Lombard is a useful direct comparator for HP, equipment leasing, residual value leasing, asset refinancing, vehicles, machinery, technology assets, wholesale stocking and renewable assets. It is not a like-for-like reference for all Frontier pages. Loans, invoice finance, trade finance, acquisition debt, ABL, property and equity require appropriate bank, specialist lender or investor references for the next rewrite.

Lombard's strengths: clear product definitions; concrete assets and audiences; named structures and how they differ; visible benefits; practical end-of-term information; product-specific next actions; and, where it controls the offering, pricing or application information. Frontier should borrow the editorial discipline, not lender-specific claims, figures, processes, tax outcomes or commitments.

Lombard is not uniformly a copy standard to reproduce. Its retrieved contract-hire page contained offers with expiry dates before this review; its commercial-vehicle page contained dated emissions-zone wording. Verify current facts independently. In particular, do not reuse advertised prices, approval speed, supplier access, geography, maintenance packages or return guarantees as Frontier terms.

## Page-by-page assessment

### 1. Funding Solutions directory — /solutions

Verdict: useful navigation, weak introduction to the offer. Priority: high.

The ordered product cards are the strongest part. The hero explains how to use a page, rather than clearly saying what Frontier helps arrange. Goal cards repeat another navigation layer and the specialist section has a vague headline. The user should immediately understand business borrowing, asset finance, property and equity, and that Frontier arranges introductions.

Rewrite: a concise product-range proposition, short meaningful product summaries and one route for people who need help choosing. Keep the directory short; detail belongs on product pages. Preserve customer goals as a secondary route, not a second competing catalogue. Comparator: Lombard finance options for explanation hierarchy, adapted for a broker/introducer.

### 2. Business Loans & Working Capital — /solutions/business-loans

Verdict: decent three-way outline, insufficient decision support. Priority: high.

Term loans, revolving facilities and overdrafts are named, but the visitor must still infer when each makes sense. Benefits such as funding a contract, spreading expansion expenditure or managing seasonal cash needs need tangible examples. The page does not clearly explain committed/uncommitted availability, what the initial borrowing conversation involves, or how assessment differs from a quote.

Rewrite: a concise definition and comparison table covering purpose, drawing funds, repayment and renewal/demand risk; examples of one-off versus recurring needs; cost components; security/guarantees; realistic information checklist. Explain secured/unsecured without implying no personal exposure. Lombard has no direct equivalent reviewed; benchmark against business-bank lending material, not equipment HP.

### 3. Asset & Equipment Finance — /solutions/asset-finance

Verdict: functional hub that feels generic. Priority: high.

Its buy/use/refinance distinction works, and the five links are correctly ordered. It lacks a convincing inventory of what clients might finance: construction plant, lifting equipment, access platforms, materials-handling assets, production machinery and business vehicles, stated as examples to assess rather than guaranteed eligibility. The visitor cannot quickly compare HP, finance lease and residual value structures.

Rewrite: lead with the business benefit; add a concise asset-category section and an ownership/use/refinance comparison. Use separate operator, hire-business and dealer examples. Keep vendor finance a seller-facing arrangement, not a borrower product. Comparator: Lombard machinery and finance-options pages.

### 4. Hire Purchase — /solutions/hire-purchase

Verdict: one of the better starting points, but underdeveloped commercially. Priority: high.

The ownership headline works, and final payments and conditional sale are covered. It does not clearly lead with a complete plain-English definition, including legal ownership during the term. It says little about predictable payments, potential fixed/variable structures or how deposit, term and a balloon change the payment profile. The copy lacks a simple purchase journey and example.

Rewrite: what HP is; why choose it; suitable assets; deposit and payment structure; ownership and responsibilities; fixed versus variable where offered; balloon trade-off; HP versus lease. Add an illustrative non-priced purchase sequence rather than invented representative terms. Lombard's definition, feature explanations and application stages are stronger; its own fee, rate, eligibility and term promises cannot be transferred.

### 5. Equipment Leasing — /solutions/equipment-leasing

Verdict: the weakest explanation of a central product choice. Priority: high.

The page puts finance lease and operating/residual value arrangements into a few sentences. It does not explain the principal economic differences with enough clarity. Most of the surrounding material asks the customer to check terms without first making the options understandable. Continued hire, third-party sale, potential rental rebates and return conditions need product-specific explanations where applicable.

Rewrite: a side-by-side comparison of finance lease, residual value/operating structures and HP. Explain who owns the asset, how residual value can affect rentals, maintenance, usage, end options and what is not automatic. State that labels and accounting classification are not interchangeable. Lombard uses separate lease and residual-value pages; our combined page must provide equivalent clarity or link to proper dedicated pages.

### 6. Asset Refinance — /solutions/asset-refinance

Verdict: correct outline, benefit and net-cash mechanics are too abstract. Priority: high.

The important original-value versus current-equity distinction is present. However, the page needs to make continued operational use and the potential uses of released cash more concrete. Sale and HP back and sale and leaseback are named, but not properly compared. Refinancing an existing loan and raising fresh cash from an owned asset are different scenarios.

Rewrite: two routes, two ownership outcomes; example flow from valuation through debt settlement and costs to net release; requirements around title and existing security; relevant assets and business uses. Explain any specialist secured-on-asset loan only where appropriate to confirmed access. Comparator: Lombard refinance page. Do not adopt its published decision target.

### 7. Vehicle & Fleet Finance — /solutions/vehicle-finance

Verdict: too broad and the thinnest new page in useful product detail. Priority: high.

It is largely three brief product descriptions. Cars/vans, commercial vehicles and businesses renting vehicles to others need clearer separation. The production-floor hero is poorly matched. Mileage, condition and maintenance are mentioned but the purchase-versus-return decision is not made easy.

Rewrite: asset/use categories; HP versus lease versus contract hire table; ownership intention; initial rental, mileage, return conditions and optional maintenance explained; whole-life costs and replacement planning. Separate operational fleets from vehicle-rental business facilities. Comparator: Lombard contract hire and commercial vehicles. No copying of offers, sourcing promises or fleet-management service claims.

### 8. Invoice Finance — /solutions/invoice-finance

Verdict: useful definitions but inadequate explanation of the cash cycle. Priority: high.

The page distinguishes credit-control roles, but never fully walks a customer through an advance, customer payment, fees and release of the remaining balance. It under-explains recourse, confidentiality and the gap between turnover and eligible invoices. It needs a better description of which trading situations it may suit.

Rewrite: a clear invoice-to-cash flow; factoring versus discounting table; practical examples of eligible/disputed/retention-affected invoices; concentration and debtor ageing; costs; collections; recourse and optional protection. Explain selective invoice funding only after confirming relevant access. No Lombard equivalent reviewed; use invoice-finance provider and British Business Bank references.

### 9. Stock & Dealer Finance — /solutions/stock-dealer-finance

Verdict: promising and commercially relevant, but reads like an outline. Priority: high.

The dealer-versus-rental distinction is good. Curtailments is unexplained jargon. The customer cannot picture funding a stock unit, demonstrating it, selling it and clearing the finance. Benefits around preserving purchasing capacity and holding sale inventory are not expressed strongly enough.

Rewrite: stock-unit lifecycle; eligible category examples; new/used/demo distinctions; holding periods and staged repayment in plain language; stock checks and security release; dealer versus manufacturer arrangements. Comparator: Lombard wholesale finance, which is more specific on audiences, unit-based funding and operation. Do not imply Frontier provides its software or stocking service.

### 10. Trade & Import Finance — /solutions/trade-import-finance

Verdict: three different concepts are introduced too quickly. Priority: high.

Supplier-payment funding, import loans, letters of credit and guarantees are not the same product. Presenting them as three cards risks leaving a novice more confused. The page correctly distinguishes importing resale stock from buying for one's own fleet, but needs a proper transaction example.

Rewrite: separate funding from risk/payment instruments; explain the supplier/shipment/delivery/customer-payment sequence; evidence and timing; pre-shipment/deposit boundaries; currencies and delays; goods for resale versus own use. Confirm letters-of-credit/guarantee introduction capability before presenting them as Frontier options. No direct Lombard equivalent; use trade-bank sources.

### 11. Acquisition & Buyout Finance — /solutions/acquisition-finance

Verdict: sounds like transaction preparation rather than a funding proposition. Priority: medium-high.

It names acquisitions and MBOs but gives limited insight into why funding structure changes across them. The distinction between acquisition debt and equity is useful but thin. Missing practical depth includes existing debt repayment, transaction costs, post-completion working capital, buyer contribution, seller funding and cash-flow/asset-led structures.

Rewrite: acquisition versus MBO cases; sources-and-uses outline; cash-flow lending versus ABL versus equity and agreed seller funding; integration and working capital; what Frontier arranges and what separate advisers do. No direct Lombard comparison; use acquisition specialists.

### 12. Asset Based Lending — /solutions/asset-based-lending

Verdict: under-explains the product's defining feature. Priority: high.

The list of asset classes is useful, but borrowing base, reserves and advance rates are introduced without enough explanation. It should demonstrate how combined assets affect availability and why that differs from one equipment loan. The current result is technically flavoured but not particularly accessible.

Rewrite: a clear combined-facility diagram or table; variable invoice/stock availability versus any term components; net proceeds; monitoring; security priorities; when complexity can be justified. No invented advances or deal minimums. No direct Lombard equivalent reviewed; use ABL specialist sources.

### 13. Finance for Your Customers — /solutions/vendor-finance

Verdict: too cautious to function as a seller proposition. Priority: high.

It accurately calls vendor finance an arrangement, but devotes more energy to what cannot be assumed than to how a seller might benefit. It needs a practical sales workflow and clear boundaries between seller, Frontier and provider. Do not say it increases conversion or guarantees faster payment without evidence.

Rewrite: seller audience; potential benefits of an additional purchase route; enquiry, assessment, documentation, delivery/acceptance and payout stages; seller/buyer/provider responsibilities; information sharing; repeat transaction setup subject to agreement. Lombard stocking is adjacent, not proof of a Frontier vendor programme. Obtain a direct vendor-programme reference for the rewrite.

### 14. Business Funding — /solutions/business-funding

Verdict: long and now partly redundant with the new directory and loans page. Priority: medium.

The current page includes much of the old process-heavy template. It risks making visitors choose between Business Funding and Business Loans without knowing the difference. Its generic illustration and repeated service/process blocks add length without product depth.

Rewrite as a short goal-led hub for people who know the cash requirement but not the product. Preserve working-capital and growth anchors. Link directly to loans, invoice finance, stock/trade, acquisition and refinance. Do not maintain a second long generic product page. No need to force a Lombard comparison.

### 15. Bridging Loans — /solutions/bridging-loans

Verdict: comparatively more developed, but needs sharper product boundaries. Priority: medium-high.

The defined-gap and exit framing works. Property-backed bridging, general short-term business finance and a bridge to a funding round should not appear interchangeable. A planned equity round can be much less certain than a contracted receipt; the page needs to explain the distinction without losing the business-bridging remit.

Rewrite: temporary need and repayment source first; separate possible structures; meaningful examples; cost, minimum interest periods and extension exposure as applicable; alternative if the need is permanent. Do not promise an unsecured bridge to any startup round. No direct Lombard equivalent; specialist bridging/short-term debt references required.

### 16. Property Finance — /solutions/property-finance

Verdict: long, but the main product families are compressed into one paragraph. Priority: high.

Commercial mortgages, property refinance and development finance deserve their own useful explanations. The generic Frontier value paragraph could appear on almost any page. The illustrative programme discussion adds process but not enough practical product choice.

Rewrite: clear owner-occupied versus investment requirement; acquisition, refinance, development and exit options; equity contribution and valuation concepts in plain terms; staged drawdown; rental/trading/sale repayment sources. Retain business scope. No direct Lombard equivalent reviewed.

### 17. Equity & Growth Capital — /solutions/growth-and-ownership-capital

Verdict: stronger fundamentals, insufficient specificity about the investor proposition. Priority: medium.

Primary versus secondary proceeds, dilution and governance are useful. More needs to be said about business maturity, capital purpose and alignment, while avoiding an unverified claim of access to specific investor types. Generic process and preparation blocks reduce the impact.

Rewrite: growth, shareholder liquidity and ownership-transition journeys; where money goes; minority/control and governance; what evidence supports an introduction; actual engagement deliverables. Keep distinct from debt-funded acquisitions and venture. Lombard is not a relevant benchmark for equity.

### 18. Venture Funding — /solutions/venture-funding

Verdict: thoughtful but dominated by readiness and milestone language. Priority: medium.

It does not clearly explain the range from initial validation to later commercial scale, or the practical material behind each conversation. It should not imply all businesses with a pitch fit venture or that an investor list is a service outcome.

Rewrite: stage-led needs and evidence; runway and next milestone; use of funds; investor fit and ownership; illustrative preparation checklist. Differentiate advisory preparation from introduction and from the investor's decision. No Lombard equivalent.

### 19. Complex Funding — /solutions/complex-funding

Verdict: a routing and triage page, not a product page; currently reads as reluctance to engage. Priority: medium.

Retain clear boundaries around viability and specialist advice, but do not lead most sections with reasons funding may fail. Name concrete circumstances such as facility maturity, lender withdrawal, a carve-out or competing security interests. It should route to actual products after establishing facts.

Rewrite shorter: situation, deadline, underlying cash need, existing obligations, relevant funding routes and specialist dependencies. Avoid suggesting emergency funding or restructuring expertise not established. No direct Lombard equivalent.

### 20. Technology & Equipment Finance — /solutions/technology-funding

Verdict: substantial overlap with Asset & Equipment Finance; still too abstract. Priority: high.

The software/implementation boundary is useful, but the page needs an explicit role centred on technology investment by operating businesses. Repeated equipment language and the detour into startup capital make the proposition fuzzy. Lombard gives technology assets a more recognisable setting and product connections.

Rewrite as Technology Finance (preserve URL initially): hardware, systems and eligible software; cost categories; asset lease versus other business funding; refresh/replacement and implementation timing. Link briefly to the Technology sector for company fundraising. Do not imply subscriptions or every implementation cost can be financed.

### 21. Sustainability & Impact — /solutions/sustainability-and-impact

Verdict: two audiences and financing models compete on one page. Priority: high.

Financing solar, storage or cleaner equipment is different from equity for an impact-led business. The current page centres impact evidence more than the investment a business wants to make. Lombard's renewable asset categories are much more concrete.

Rewrite: two explicit routes: sustainable business investment and capital for impact-led growth. Lead the asset route with concrete investments, useful commercial questions and implementation assumptions. Keep intended outcomes/evidence in proportion. Retain impact capital as a separate subsection or linked route; no promised grants, discounts or savings. Comparator: Lombard renewables only for the asset/project side, not impact equity.

## Accuracy, credibility and commercial limits

- The review found weak explanation and positioning, not evidence that every product description is false. Specific product access still requires provider agreements.
- Do not infer a verified panel, whole-of-market comparison, exclusive pricing, best rates, speed or an approval probability from the existence of product pages.
- Explain product-specific alternatives in plain English. Do not treat every lease as the same or every stock use as a rental fleet.
- Publish verified ranges, fees or times only when they apply to Frontier's actual channel. Until then, useful product mechanics and non-priced illustrations can supply depth.
- Use illustrative scenarios labelled as such, not fictional case studies or invented results.
- Keep necessary information on risks and fees. Remove redundant repetitions; never remove a material qualification solely to strengthen sales language.
- Customer-facing provider references and logos are separate decisions; internal research does not establish an endorsement or partnership.

## Rewrite standard and priorities

First priority: equipment hub, HP, leasing, refinance, vehicle, invoice, stock and vendor pages. These underpin the plant/machinery proposition. Then loans, ABL, trade, property and acquisition. Finally simplify the overlapping hubs and refine equity, venture and specialist routes.

For each detailed product page: a direct definition near the top; a recognisable customer need; three to five specific benefits or use cases; suitable asset/transaction examples; mechanics that explain the movement of money and ownership; a comparison with the closest alternative; one relevant non-priced illustration where it helps; proportionate commitments; product-specific Frontier support; substantive FAQs; and an actionable enquiry invitation. A hub should be shorter and help choose. A specialist route should describe its situation and link to products.

Avoid arbitrary word targets. Additional detail must answer a customer question. Use product-specific headings and layouts where a comparison table or cash-cycle sequence works better than another identical three-card section. Maintain Frontier's clear, calm British-English voice. Benefits should be factual and conditional where necessary, not merely enthusiastic.
